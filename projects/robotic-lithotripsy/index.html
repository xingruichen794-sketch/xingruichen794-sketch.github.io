The research question

As a laser creates a crater, the distance from its fiber tip to the stone changes. This study asks whether controlled, stepwise adjustment of that standoff distance can improve stone dusting relative to maintaining a fixed position.

Experiments used submerged BegoStone samples and a Ho:YAG laser under benchtop conditions. The study evaluates ablation geometry rather than clinical outcomes.

What I built and analyzed

  • Robotic experiment platform. Constructed the benchtop setup around a DOBOT Nova 5 arm and implemented controlled positioning of the laser fiber relative to the stone.
  • Motion strategies. Compared fixed-distance baselines with two-step and four-step adjustment strategies across selected initial distances and pulse counts.
  • OCT analysis pipeline. Developed MATLAB processing to segment lithotripsy craters and quantify volume, depth, and profile area.
  • Outcome comparison. Compared crater geometry across conditions to examine where dynamic control was useful and where it offered limited benefit.

A condition-dependent improvement

4-stepstrategy vs. fixed-distance baseline

At an initial standoff of 0.75 mm, the four-step strategy increased crater volume by 34.4%, 27.1%, and 44.5% at 80, 160, and 320 pulses, respectively, compared with the fixed 0.75 mm condition. The report identifies these differences as statistically significant.

Dynamic adjustment did not provide a consistent advantage when the initial standoff was already 0.5 mm. The result is specific to the tested benchtop conditions and does not establish clinical effectiveness.

What the result suggests

The experiments support studying standoff control as a way to influence ablation morphology. The report associates crater-volume changes more strongly with changes in profile area than with average depth.

Further work includes broader experimental validation, repeated baseline comparisons, and control strategies that combine axial adjustment with lateral scanning.
